===== ppt/slides/slide10.xml =====


The Problem: Removing the MCU Without Losing I2C

Reference: SenSys '24 Paper, Sections 1-2 (architecture and motivation)
Now: every sensor node carries its own MCU, radio and firmware — cost and code size scale with the node count
Proposal: share a few processors, reduce the rest to RF peripherals
Lite node = sensor chips + RF front-end + 5 logic components — no MCU, no firmware
Three things break once the MCU is gone:
PHY — who encodes and decodes I2C bytes over the air?
Duplex — is this LOW the master's, or my own echo?
Addressing — who is being addressed, without an address parser?
One design move per blocker — the next three slides
10
===== ppt/slides/slide11.xml =====


Problem 1 → Solution: An Open-Drain Radio

Reference: SenSys '24 Paper, Sections 3.1-3.2, Figures 3-4
Problem: no firmware — a lite node cannot run framing, encoding or bit sync
Problem: it must still listen around the clock, so the receiver has to be passive
Solution: exploit I2C's own asymmetry — only LOW is ever driven, HIGH is the pull-up
Carrier ON = pull LOW, silence = release: no bit encoding needed at all
Any carrier pulls every node low — I2C's wired-AND survives as 'wireless-NOR'
Envelope detector (SAW + Schottky) listens at near-zero power, both ends symmetric
11
===== ppt/slides/slide12.xml =====


Problem 2 → Solution: A Half-Duplex Deadlock Fix

Reference: SenSys '24 Paper, Section 4.1, Figure 5
Problem: a lite node cannot tell whether a LOW came from the master or from itself
Problem: a naive echo clamps both buses LOW forever — the link deadlocks
Solution: let the only side that has a processor resolve the ambiguity
An SPDT switch on the processor, driven by the I2C phase it already generates
Two carrier frequencies keep the two directions apart
The lite node gains zero parts — complexity moves up to the cluster head
12
===== ppt/slides/slide13.xml =====


Problem 3 → Solution: Addressing Without a Stack

Reference: SenSys '24 Paper, Section 4.2, Figures 7-8
Problem: thousands of chips share one medium, and parsing an I2C address needs an FSM — i.e. firmware
Solution: put the address before START, where the sensor chips cannot see it
2-byte header: session length + interface address
I2C ignores everything before START — the frame stays standard
Decoder = 5 logic components: shift register, counter, 2×XNOR, D-latch (+ DIP switch)
2,048-chip address space; ~24,780 chips/s at 1 Hz polling
No address parser, no START/STOP state machine — just 'is it my address?' and a countdown.
13
===== ppt/slides/slide14.xml =====


Outcome: What It Buys and What It Costs

Reference: SenSys '24 Paper, Sections 3-7 and Section 9 (Discussion)
Gain: 120 µJ per 2-byte transfer (1/160 of LoRa), 6.1-6.7× system energy, −33.5% node price
Gain: 96 m LOS range at 50 kbps; 3.4× faster response via one aggregated uplink
Cost: range collapses with rate — 23 m at 200 kbps, 5 m NLOS
Cost: 95% of a lite node is its RFBus interface → saving caps at ~41%
Cost: single point of failure, 3 RF chains, I2C-only (73% of digital sensors)
It doesn't make nodes cheaper by making them smarter —
it moves the cost to a few cluster heads.

After reviewing the results, ask why this design suits periodic sensing. Batching spreads the overhead across several readings.
